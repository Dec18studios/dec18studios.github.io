Why it exists

Great idea, locked interface.

Going from HDR to SDR with a Dolby Vision sidecar sounds great on paper. The problem is the control surface inside Resolve: it keeps you out of the values that drive the whole thing, the Min, Max and Average.

You can run a frame analysis, a clip analysis, a shot analysis, any of the options it hands you. What you can't do is say exactly what those three numbers should be.

Treated as a theoretical DRT or CST, Dolby Vision taking HDR to SDR shouldn't change its behavior clip to clip or shot to shot. It should be one consistent mapping. This DCTL is how you get that.

Ways people actually use it
One mapping for a whole film
Set a single Min, Max and Average for the show and apply it to every shot, so the HDR to SDR path behaves like a fixed transform rather than a per-shot decision.
Sanity-check the trim
Hold the values steady and change nothing else, so anything you see moving is your grade, not the analysis.
Learn what the numbers do
Push Max up and down on the same frame to feel how much of the SDR result is coming from metadata rather than the image.
Match a delivery spec
When a deliverable calls for specific L1 values, type them in instead of hunting for a shot whose analysis happens to land there.

Every control

Three numbers, and that's the tool.

There is nothing else to learn. The DCTL exists to put the L1 values under your hand, so the control set is as small as it can be.

ControlWhat it doesSet it
Min The minimum luminance value reported on analysis. Anchors the bottom end of the mapping instead of letting the darkest pixel in the shot decide it. once, film-wide
Max The maximum luminance value reported on analysis. This is the one that moves the SDR result most, so it's the one worth auditioning. once, film-wide
Average The average luminance value reported on analysis. Effectively where the trim pivots, and the reason two similar shots can otherwise come out different. once, film-wide
