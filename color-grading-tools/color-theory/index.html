What it is

A perception lab that lives in a node.

Color Theory is a free OFX plugin that draws colour vision demonstrations procedurally on the GPU. Nothing is a bitmap, so every figure is crisp at any timeline resolution, animates cleanly, and can be pushed through your own transforms to see how they survive.

Fourteen of the modes generate their own image. Three run on whatever footage you feed the node, so you can show what a histogram move or a reduced palette really does to a picture. Two more lay down test charts, a grey reference card and an RGB chip chart, for checking a pipeline end to end.

  • Colourists who want to understand why a shot reads warm next to one surround and cold next to another.
  • Educators who need a live demonstration in class instead of a slide, with the explanation already burned into the frame.
  • Anyone teaching how colour perception works, on YouTube, in a workshop, or to a client who does not believe you.
Umbrella stripes One grey, drawn twice

Two of the rings are filled with exactly the same grey, one under the warm spokes and one under the cool ones. Each ring drifts toward the stripes crossing it, so the inner one reads warm and the outer one reads cool. Drag Reveal and they snap back together.

The three controls that matter

Show it, break it, then show the percept.

Every mode shares the same three tools, so once you learn one demonstration you can drive all of them.

Always on

Commentary overlay

Each mode burns its own explanation into the frame: what the figure is, what you think you are seeing, and what the pixels actually contain. It renders with the image, so a screen recording or an export carries the teaching with it.
The proof

Reveal

One slider wipes the surround, stripes or shadow away to a neutral grey, leaving only the patches under test. Animate it and the illusion collapses on camera, which is a far better argument than telling someone the values match.
The other side

Simulate Appearance

Flips the figure over and draws the predicted percept instead of the physical values, so you can put what your eye reports next to what the scope reports and see exactly how far apart they sit.

Generated illusions · 14

These draw their own image, so you can drop the node on any clip or a solid and it takes over the frame.

Simultaneous Contrast

Two identical patches read light or dark depending on the surround they sit in.

Gamut Expansion

A surround pushes a patch to look more saturated than the values it actually holds.

Contrast vs Expansion Lab Stimulus

A side by side stimulus that separates a contrast shift from an expansion shift.

Colour Assimilation Stripes

Overlaid stripes drag a single background colour toward the stripe hue.

Transparent Filter

A patch that reads as a sheet of coloured film laid over the image beneath.

Pinwheel Transparency

A rotating wedge figure where the same values flip between layer and surface.

Contrast as Transparency

Lower local contrast alone is enough to make a region read as veiled.

Umbrella Stripes

Stripe geometry over a field, where the crossing pattern rewrites the colour you report.

Checker Shadow

A cast shadow on a checkerboard makes two identical squares look nothing alike.

Coloured Holes

Apertures cut through a surface, where the surround decides the colour you see in the gap.

Gradient Lightness

A flat bar laid on a gradient appears to ramp even though it never changes.

Shared Texture Discs

Discs sharing one texture, read as different materials depending on their context.

Overlapping Squares

Two squares that overlap, where the join tells you which one is in front and what colour it is.

Perceived Luminance Visualizer

A hue sweep behind grey ramp shutters. Where the border melts away, the colour and the grey share one luminance. Markers, a swatch with the percentage inside it and a live readout show the number. Set Shutter Count to 0 for a plain sweep with just the probes. Keyframe the hue and watch them ride the ramp.

Passthru illusions · 3

These run on the footage already in the node, so you can demonstrate the point on a real shot rather than a diagram.

Histogram Compression

Squeeze the range of the picture and watch how much of it your eye puts straight back.

Two Colour Method

Rebuild the shot from two colour records and see how much of a full picture survives.

Palette Reconstruction

Reduce the image to a small palette and show how few colours a picture really needs.

Pipeline test charts · 2

Known references you push through the whole chain. The four photographs ship inside the plugin as scene linear frames and leave the node untouched by default, no clamp and no encode. Put a Colour Space Transform after the node, tell it the stored space, and judge what your pipeline does to the hard parts. The commentary on each one says what to look for.

Grey Reference Card

A clean grey card with a chip row, for checking exposure, neutrality and transform behaviour.

RGB Chips

Hue rows against exposure columns that double around mid grey, the same chart as the RGB Chips DCTL with every control: hues, columns, saturation, clamp, gray ramp, gaps, HSV or spherical model, continuous modes. Values leave the node raw, so hang a CST after it and watch what moves.

Getting started

Install in a couple of steps.

Color Theory is a standard OFX bundle. Drop it in your host's OFX plugins folder and restart. Or let the Tool Box Manager handle it along with everything else.

Download your platform build

Grab the macOS, Windows or Linux build from the Downloads above, then unzip the .zip.

Move it into your OFX plugins folder

macOS: /Library/OFX/Plugins · Windows: C:\Program Files\Common Files\OFX\Plugins · Linux: /usr/OFX/Plugins. The final layout is /OFX/Plugins/ColorTheory.ofx.bundle/.

Restart your host

Relaunch Resolve, Fusion or Nuke so it picks up the new plugin, and make sure OFX is enabled.

Drop it on a node and pick a mode

Find Color Theory under the Dec. 18 Studios OFX group. Choose a group, choose a mode, then drive Reveal and Simulate Appearance to make your point.

Heads up · macOS Gatekeeper

On macOS, if Apple tells you to delete it, don't. Open System Settings → Privacy & Security, choose Allow Anyway for Color Theory, then restart Resolve. The plugin is unsigned, not unsafe.
